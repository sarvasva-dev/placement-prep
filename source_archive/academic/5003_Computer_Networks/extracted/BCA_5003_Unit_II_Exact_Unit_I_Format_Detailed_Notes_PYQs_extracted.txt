1. Unit-II Roadmap
Unit II explains how signals physically travel from one network device to another and what limits their speed and quality. It is
the foundation for understanding the OSI Physical Layer, LAN cabling, Wi-Fi, satellite links, signal quality, and data-rate
numericals.
Study order for maximum marks
1. First learn guided and unguided media classification.
2. Then master each cable’s structure, working, advantages, disadvantages, and applications.
3. Learn radio propagation, microwave line-of-sight communication, and satellite terms.
4. Understand the three impairments: attenuation, distortion, and noise.

6. Finally, write all PYQs from memory under timed conditions.

2. Transmission Media: Meaning and Classification
Transmission media are the channels through which data signals travel from a sender to a receiver. The signal may be
electrical in copper cable, optical in fibre cable, or electromagnetic in wireless/free-space communication. The choice of
medium affects data capacity, transmission distance, attenuation, interference, cost, installation, security, and mobility.

Guided media provide a controlled physical route. They are commonly preferred for fixed installations such as offices,
computer laboratories, and data centres. Unguided media support mobility and places where cabling is difficult or expensive,

3. Guided Transmission Media

Guided media use cables or fibres as a physical path. Copper cables carry electrical signals. Optical fibre carries modulated
light pulses. The major guided media are twisted-pair cable, coaxial cable, and optical fibre.
3.1 Twisted-Pair Cable
A twisted-pair cable contains two insulated copper conductors twisted around each other. Twisting reduces noise pickup and
crosstalk because external electromagnetic interference tends to affect both conductors similarly; differential signalling can
reject much of this common interference. Standard Ethernet LAN cable commonly contains four twisted pairs enclosed
inside one outer jacket.

UTP — Unshielded Twisted Pair
UTP consists of insulated twisted copper pairs with no metallic shield. It is lightweight, flexible, inexpensive, easy to
terminate, and widely used in Ethernet LANs and telephone cabling. Modern categories such as Cat5e, Cat6, Cat6A, and
beyond are designed for different frequency/performance requirements.
Advantages: low cost; flexible; easy installation; readily available; suitable for voice and LAN data.
Limitations: copper is vulnerable to electromagnetic interference, crosstalk, and attenuation; cable-distance limits apply.
STP — Shielded Twisted Pair
STP includes twisted pairs plus metallic foil/braid shielding. The shield helps reduce electromagnetic interference and
crosstalk in noisy electrical environments, such as industrial sites or locations near heavy machinery. Shielding should be
properly grounded to work effectively.
Advantages: better EMI protection and potentially better performance in electrically noisy locations.
Limitations: thicker, heavier, less flexible, more expensive, and more difficult to install than UTP.

3.2 Coaxial Cable
Coaxial cable has two conductors sharing a common axis. The central conductor carries the signal; a dielectric insulator
surrounds it; then an outer conducting shield surrounds the dielectric; a protective jacket covers the cable. The shield
protects the inner signal from external interference and confines the electromagnetic field.

Optical fibre is a strand of very pure glass or plastic that carries information as pulses of light. It offers exceptionally high
bandwidth, low attenuation, low bit-error probability, electrical isolation, and immunity to electromagnetic interference. It is
widely used in telecom backbone networks, data centres, fibre broadband, and long-distance communication.

Working principle: the core has a slightly higher refractive index than the cladding. When light in the core strikes the
core–cladding boundary at an angle greater than the critical angle, it undergoes total internal reflection and remains
guided within the core. This is the correct scientific explanation of light guidance in optical fibre.

Advantages of optical fibre
• Very high bandwidth and support for very high data rates.
• Low attenuation, so longer distances are possible with fewer repeaters/regenerators.
• Immunity to electromagnetic interference, radio-frequency interference, and crosstalk.
• Better physical security because fibre does not radiate electromagnetic signals like copper; however, no medium is
absolutely impossible to tap.
• Small diameter, low weight, electrical isolation, and no spark/fire risk from signal current.
• High reliability and potentially low long-term operational cost for large-capacity links.
Limitations of optical fibre
• Higher initial equipment, connector, and installation cost than basic copper cabling.
• Fibre is mechanically more delicate and requires skilled splicing/termination and specialised test equipment.
• It cannot deliver electrical power to endpoint devices as copper Ethernet can with Power over Ethernet.
4. Unguided Transmission Media
Unguided media transmit electromagnetic signals through air, vacuum, or free space without a physical cable between
sender and receiver. They are used when mobility, broad coverage, rapid deployment, or difficult terrain makes wired
infrastructure less suitable.

Radio waves are electromagnetic waves used for broadcasting, mobile communication, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, wireless LANs,
and many other services. Depending on frequency and environment, radio signals may propagate as ground waves, sky
waves, or line-of-sight waves.

4.2 Microwave Communication
Microwave communication uses high-frequency radio signals, commonly with directional dish antennas. Terrestrial
microwave links generally require line of sight. Because obstacles and Earth curvature restrict range, repeaters/relay towers
may be positioned between end stations. Microwave is used for point-to-point communication, cellular backhaul, TV
distribution, radar, and satellite uplink/downlink links.

4.3 Infrared
Infrared communication uses infrared electromagnetic radiation for short-range data transfer. It usually does not penetrate
walls well, so it is confined to a room or direct path. This feature reduces interference across rooms but limits coverage.
Examples include TV remote controls and short-range optical links.
4.4 Satellite
Satellite communication is an unguided medium in which a satellite acts as a relay/transponder in space. It connects distant
Earth stations using uplink and downlink radio paths. Detailed notes and PYQs are given in the next section.
5. Satellite Communication
Satellite communication transfers information between distant locations using an artificial satellite placed in orbit around
Earth. The satellite acts as a repeater in space. Ground stations send a signal upward to the satellite, and the satellite
transponder processes and retransmits it toward the required ground region.

6. Transmission Impairment
Transmission impairment means deterioration or alteration of a signal as it travels through a communication medium. The
signal received at the destination may not have exactly the same strength, shape, timing, or information content as the
transmitted signal. For digital data, impairment can cause bit errors; for analog signals, it reduces quality.
The three major types are attenuation, distortion, and noise.
6.1 Attenuation
Attenuation is the gradual reduction in signal strength as distance increases. It is commonly measured in decibels (dB). If a
signal becomes too weak compared with background noise, the receiver may misinterpret bits or lose the signal. Digital
repeaters regenerate signals; amplifiers increase signal power but can also amplify noise.

Distortion occurs when different frequency components of a composite signal experience different attenuation or

6.3 Noise
Noise is unwanted electrical or electromagnetic energy that becomes mixed with the desired signal. It is random and
unpredictable. It cannot be removed completely, but good design can reduce its impact. Noise lowers signal-to-noise ratio
(SNR), increases bit errors, and limits maximum reliable data rate.

7. Data-Rate Limits: Nyquist and Shannon
Data rate is the number of bits transmitted per second and is measured in bps. The maximum possible rate is not unlimited.
It depends on available bandwidth, signal levels, and channel quality/noise. Two fundamental formulas describe theoretical
limits: Nyquist for an ideal noiseless channel and Shannon for a noisy real channel.
Term
Meaning

Bit rate
Number of data bits sent per second; measured in bps.

Number of symbols/signal elements transmitted per second. A symbol can carry one or more bits.

Number of distinct signal states used to represent data.
SNR
Signal power divided by noise power.
7.1 Nyquist Bit-Rate Formula: Noiseless Channel
For an ideal channel without noise, Nyquist gives the maximum bit rate:
Maximum bit rate = 2B logI(L) bps
Where B is bandwidth in hertz and L is the number of signal levels. Larger bandwidth permits higher bit rate. More levels can
theoretically carry more bits per symbol, but in real channels many levels are harder to distinguish and reduce reliability.
Solved example 1
Given B = 3000 Hz and L = 2
Bit rate = 2 × 3000 × logI(2)
= 6000 × 1
= 6000 bps
Solved example 2
Given B = 3000 Hz and L = 8
Bit rate = 2 × 3000 × logI(8)
= 6000 × 3
= 18,000 bps
7.2 Shannon Capacity: Noisy Channel
Every practical communication channel has noise. Shannon’s theorem gives the highest theoretical reliable data rate for a
noisy channel:

Capacity = B logI(1 + S/N) bps
Where B is bandwidth in Hz and S/N is the linear signal-to-noise power ratio. Unlike Nyquist, Shannon’s limit does not
contain the number of signal levels. It is a fundamental upper limit imposed by the channel’s bandwidth and noise.
Solved example 3
Given B = 3000 Hz and S/N = 1000
Capacity = 3000 × logI(1 + 1000)
= 3000 × logI(1001)
≈ 3000 × 9.967
≈ 29,900 bps
Solved example 4: SNR given in dB
Given B = 3000 Hz and SNR = 30 dB
First convert: S/N = 10^(30/10) = 10³ = 1000
Then: Capacity = 3000 × logI(1001) ≈ 29,900 bps

Q1. Mention the advantages of optical fibre cables.
Asked: 2025–26
Answer
Optical fibre is a guided communication medium that transmits data as pulses of light through a glass or plastic core. It is
widely used for high-speed and long-distance communication because of its superior performance compared with copper
cable.
1. High bandwidth: Optical fibre can carry very large volumes of information at high speed. It is suitable for broadband
Internet, telecommunications, high-definition video, cloud services, and data centres.
2. Low attenuation: Light signals experience very low loss in fibre. Therefore, communication can be extended over long
distances with fewer repeaters/regenerators than copper systems.
3. Immunity to electromagnetic interference: Fibre does not carry electrical current. It is not affected by electromagnetic
interference, radio-frequency interference, lightning-induced noise, or crosstalk like copper cables.
4. Better security: Fibre does not radiate electromagnetic signals in the same way as copper. Unauthorised tapping is more
difficult and normally requires physical access to the cable, making fibre relatively secure.
5. Small size and light weight: Fibre cables are thin and light, so a large capacity can be installed in limited duct space.
6. Electrical isolation and safety: Fibre is non-conductive. It does not create spark hazards or ground loops and is suitable
in high-voltage or electrically noisy environments.
7. High reliability and low error rate: Its EMI immunity and low attenuation support high-quality transmission with low bit
error probability.
8. Lower long-term operating cost: While initial deployment can be expensive, fewer repeaters, high capacity, and
reliability can make fibre economical over time.
Conclusion: Optical fibre is the preferred medium for high-bandwidth, long-distance, secure, and interference-resistant
communication.
Q2. Explain various transmission media in detail.
Asked: 2018; related indicators: 2014, 2024–25
Answer
Transmission media are the communication paths that carry signals from a sender to a receiver. They are classified as
guided media and unguided media.
Guided media carry signals through a physical path. Twisted-pair cable uses insulated copper wires twisted together. UTP
is unshielded, economical, flexible, and common in LANs. STP includes shielding and is better in electrically noisy
environments. Coaxial cable has a central conductor, dielectric, metallic shield, and outer jacket; it offers good noise
immunity and is used in cable TV, CCTV, and RF systems. Optical fibre carries light through a core and cladding by total
internal reflection; it provides very high bandwidth, low attenuation, long distance, security, and immunity to EMI.
Unguided media transmit electromagnetic waves through free space. Radio waves are used for broadcasting and wireless
networking. Microwaves are directional and usually line-of-sight; they are used in terrestrial point-to-point links and satellite
communication. Infrared is short-range and generally does not penetrate walls. Satellite communication uses a satellite
transponder to relay signals between distant Earth stations through uplink and downlink paths.
Conclusion: Guided media are appropriate for fixed, controlled installations; unguided media are suitable for mobility, wide
coverage, and hard-to-cable regions. Medium selection depends on bandwidth, distance, cost, interference, security, and
mobility requirements.

Q4. What is satellite? Explain satellite communication with a proper diagram.
Asked: 2022–23; satellite networks: 2023–24
Answer
A communication satellite is an artificial satellite equipped with transponders that receives radio signals from an Earth
station and retransmits them to another Earth station. It works as a relay/repeater in space and enables communication over
very large geographical distances.
Diagram:
Earth station A → Uplink → Satellite transponder (receive → filter → frequency conversion → amplification → transmit) →
Downlink → Earth station B.
Working: The sender Earth station sends an uplink signal to the satellite. The satellite transponder receives it, filters it,
usually changes its frequency to separate receive and transmit channels, amplifies it, and sends it back to the coverage area
as a downlink. A receiving Earth station receives and demodulates the downlink.
Terms: Uplink means Earth-to-satellite transmission; downlink means satellite-to-Earth transmission; a footprint is the
satellite coverage area; a GEO satellite appears stationary because it orbits Earth at the same angular speed as Earth’s
rotation.
Applications: TV/radio broadcast, remote Internet, telephone, weather and navigation services, military, aviation, maritime
communication, and disaster response.
Advantages: wide coverage, remote-area connectivity, broadcast capability, and deployment where terrestrial infrastructure
is difficult.
Limitations: high cost, weather effects, and high propagation delay for GEO systems.
Conclusion: Satellite communication is essential for wide-area connectivity, especially where terrestrial links are
unavailable or impractical.
Q5. What is transmission impairment? Explain noise in signal transmission.
Asked: 2021
Answer
Transmission impairment is deterioration of a signal during its travel through a communication medium. The signal received
at the destination may not be identical to the signal transmitted. Digital signals may suffer bit errors; analog signals may lose
quality.
The main impairments are attenuation, distortion, and noise. Attenuation is loss of signal strength with distance. Distortion is
waveform change because components travel differently. Noise is an unwanted random signal added to the desired
message signal.
Noise: Noise can be introduced by electronic components, power lines, motors, nearby cables, atmospheric events, and
switching activity. It makes valid signal levels difficult to distinguish, thereby lowering SNR and increasing errors.
Types: thermal noise due to random electron motion; induced/EMI noise from electrical sources; crosstalk due to coupling
between lines; and impulse noise due to sudden high-energy events such as lightning or switching.
Effects: hiss in radio, buzz in telephone audio, flicker in television, corrupted bits in digital data, and lower maximum reliable
data rate.
Reduction methods: shielding, twisted pair, proper grounding, fibre optics, filtering, better cable routing, stronger signal
design, and error detection/correction techniques.
Conclusion: Noise cannot be eliminated completely, but it can be controlled so that the receiver can recover data reliably.

Q6. Differentiate between noiseless and noisy channels.
Asked: 2017, 2021, 2022–23
Answer
Noiseless channel: An ideal theoretical channel in which random noise is assumed absent. Nyquist theorem is used:
Maximum bit rate = 2B logIL. The rate depends on bandwidth B and signal levels L.
Noisy channel: A practical channel that contains noise. Shannon capacity is used: Capacity = B logI(1 + S/N). The rate
depends on bandwidth and signal-to-noise ratio.
Differences:
1. Noise: absent in ideal noiseless model; present in noisy channel.
2. Formula: Nyquist for noiseless; Shannon for noisy.
3. Factors: Nyquist uses B and L; Shannon uses B and S/N.
4. Reality: noiseless is an analytical assumption; all real channels have some noise.
5. Error probability: ideal noiseless model assumes no noise-caused error; noisy channels require robust signalling/coding
for reliable communication.
Q7. What are data-rate limits? On which factors do they depend? Discuss calculation for noisy and
noiseless channels.
Asked: 2015
Answer
Data-rate limit is the highest theoretical rate at which data can be transmitted reliably through a channel. It is measured in
bits per second. The major factors are available bandwidth, number of signal levels, and channel quality/noise level.
For a noiseless channel: Nyquist formula applies: Maximum bit rate = 2B logIL bps. Here B is bandwidth in Hz and L is
number of distinct signal levels. Example: if B = 3000 Hz and L = 2, rate = 2 × 3000 × logI2 = 6000 bps.
For a noisy channel: Shannon formula applies: Capacity = B logI(1 + S/N) bps. Here S/N is linear signal-to-noise ratio.
Example: B = 3000 Hz, S/N = 1000. Capacity = 3000 logI(1001) ≈ 29,900 bps.
Conclusion: Nyquist explains ideal-channel limits based on bandwidth and levels, while Shannon defines the fundamental
maximum reliable rate in a real noisy channel. Practical systems must remain within channel constraints.
Q8. Differentiate guided and unguided transmission media. Give one example of each.
Asked: 2017
Answer
Guided media carry signals through a physical path such as copper cable or optical fibre. Examples: UTP, coaxial cable,
and optical fibre. They have controlled paths, require cable installation, generally offer more stable performance, and are
physically harder to intercept.
Unguided media carry electromagnetic waves through air, vacuum, or free space. Examples: radio waves, microwave,
infrared, and satellite. They provide mobility and broad coverage but can be affected by weather, obstacles, interference,
and interception unless protected by security mechanisms.
One example: Optical fibre is guided media; microwave is unguided media.

Accuracy reminders
• Optical fibre works by total internal reflection, not by a simplistic statement that “cladding reflects light.”
• A zero-noise channel is an ideal theoretical model; practical channels always have some noise.
• Use S/N as a linear ratio in Shannon’s formula. Convert from dB before substitution.
• Bandwidth is measured in Hz; data rate is measured in bps; baud means symbols per second.
• A GEO satellite is approximately 35,786 km above the equator and introduces noticeable propagation delay.
