2. Multidimensional Analysis
Multidimensional analysis is a method of examining data from several perspectives, called dimensions. Common dimensions
are time, product, customer, region, department, sales channel, and scenario. It is widely used in data warehouses, OLAP
systems, business intelligence, forecasting, and decision-support systems.
Simple example
Suppose a company wants to analyse sales. A traditional report may show sales in rows and columns. A multidimensional
system can analyse the same sales by product, city, month, customer type, and sales channel. The manager can move from
a total to a detailed view without writing a new complex query each time.
Dimensions: Product × Region × Time Measure: Sales amount Example question: What were laptop sales in
Kanpur during January?

3. Data Mining
Data mining is the process of discovering useful, valid, new, and understandable patterns from large datasets. It uses
statistics, machine learning, artificial intelligence, database technology, and visualization.
The word mining is used as a metaphor. Just as valuable ore is extracted from a large quantity of earth, useful patterns are
extracted from a large quantity of data.

Five benefits of data mining
1. Enhanced decision-making: decisions are based on evidence instead of guesswork.
2. Predictive analysis: historical data helps forecast future trends.
3. Customer-behaviour insights: buying patterns and preferences can guide marketing.
4. Operational efficiency: data can reveal bottlenecks and predict equipment failure.
5. Fraud detection: unusual patterns can be identified in financial or transaction data.

Advantages, applications, and limitations
Advantages and applications
Data mining is used in customer relationship management, marketing, retail, banking, credit scoring, manufacturing,
healthcare, fraud detection, recommendation systems, predictive maintenance, risk management, text mining, churn
prediction, human-resource analytics, and criminal-justice analysis.
In market-basket analysis, an organization discovers that customers who buy one item often buy another item. In banking,
suspicious transaction patterns may be detected. In manufacturing, sensor data may be used to predict machine failure

Limitations
• Privacy concerns when personal, behavioural, or financial data is collected and analysed.

Statistics
Statistics is not identical to data mining, but it is an important foundation. It collects, describes, summarizes, and analyses
data. Common summary measures include maximum, minimum, mean, median, mode, and variance.
In linear regression, a model predicts a numerical value from one or more predictors. The simple equation is: Prediction = a +
b × Predictor. The selected line tries to minimize the prediction error.
Nearest neighbour and K-nearest neighbours
Nearest-neighbour prediction finds historical records that are most similar to a new record. The prediction is based on the
nearest record. K-nearest neighbours improves this by considering the K closest records and taking a majority vote for a class
or an average for a numerical value.
Confidence is higher when neighbours are very close and mostly give the same prediction. Confidence is lower when
neighbours are far away or disagree with one another.
Clustering
Clustering groups similar records together. Unlike classification, it does not start with predefined class labels. It is an
unsupervised learning method.
Decision trees
A decision tree is a predictive model shaped like a tree. Each internal node asks a question, each branch represents an
answer, and each leaf gives a class or prediction. It is popular because the result is easy to understand and explain to
business users.

Neural networks
An artificial neural network is a computer model inspired by connected biological neurons. It learns patterns from examples
and can produce highly accurate predictions, but it often requires careful data preparation, normalization, network design,
training, and overfitting control.
Rule induction
Rule induction discovers understandable if–then rules. For example: If a customer buys a printer, then the customer buys ink
with a certain confidence and support. Its strength is discovering many patterns; its weakness is that the user may receive too
many rules, including conflicting or unimportant ones.

6. KDD Process and Role of Data Mining
KDD means Knowledge Discovery in Databases. KDD is the complete process of extracting useful knowledge from data.
Data mining is one important step inside KDD, where algorithms are applied to discover patterns.
Data sources → Cleaning → Integration → Selection → Transformation → Data mining → Pattern
evaluation → Knowledge presentation
Steps of KDD
1. Data cleaning: remove noise and handle missing or inconsistent values.
2. Data integration: combine data from multiple databases or sources.
3. Data selection: choose data relevant to the analysis task.
4. Data transformation: normalize, aggregate, summarize, or convert data into a suitable form.
5. Data mining: apply intelligent techniques to discover patterns.
6. Pattern evaluation: identify patterns that are useful, valid, interesting, and understandable.
7. Knowledge presentation: communicate the discovered knowledge through reports, visualizations, rules, or dashboards.
Role of data mining in KDD
Data mining is the core analytical step of KDD. It applies classification, clustering, association, regression, rule induction,
neural networks, and other methods. However, data mining cannot replace human judgement. The analyst must understand
the business problem, the meaning of the data, and the practical value of the discovered pattern.

Data-mining implementation process
1. Business understanding: define objectives, constraints, expected results, and project plan.
2. Data understanding: collect initial data, describe it, explore it, and check quality.
3. Data preparation: select, clean, construct, integrate, and format the final dataset.
4. Modelling: select methods, create a test design, build models, and assess them.
5. Evaluation: check whether the model and process satisfy business objectives.
6. Deployment: use the model for prediction, reporting, monitoring, or a repeatable operational process.

9. Classification, Prediction, and Clustering
Classification
Classification is a supervised learning task. It assigns each data record to one of several predefined classes. The training
data contains attributes and known class labels.
Example: A bank may classify a loan application as Safe or Risky using age, income, employment, and credit history.
Classification process
1. Build the classifier: use labelled training data to learn classification rules or a model.
2. Test the classifier: apply it to test data and measure accuracy.
3. Use the classifier: classify new records when performance is acceptable.
IF income = high THEN loan decision = safe IF age = young THEN loan decision = risky IF age =
middle-aged AND income = low THEN loan decision = risky
Prediction
Prediction estimates an unknown or future value. Unlike ordinary classification, the predicted output is often continuous, such
as sales amount, income, temperature, or demand.

10. K-Means Clustering
K-means is an iterative clustering algorithm that divides data into K groups. K is selected by the user or estimated using
methods such as the elbow method.
Algorithm
1. Choose K initial centroids.
2. Assign each data point to the nearest centroid, usually using Euclidean distance.
3. Recalculate the centroid of every cluster.
4. Reassign points using the new centroids.
5. Repeat until no point changes cluster or the centroids become stable.
For one-dimensional data: Distance = |x − centroid| New centroid = sum of points in cluster / number of
points
Worked example
Data: {2, 4, 10, 12, 3, 20, 11, 25}; K = 2. To make the arithmetic transparent, choose initial centroids 4 and 11.

Limitations
• The result may not be globally optimal.
• The value of K must be selected.
• Different random initial centroids can give different results.
• It works best with roughly compact, separable clusters and may fail on complex shapes.
• It can become slow for very large datasets.
• Outliers can strongly affect centroids.

11. Outlier Detection
Outlier detection, also called anomaly detection, identifies observations that differ significantly from the rest of the data. An
outlier may be a measurement error, a rare event, fraud, or a meaningful unusual case.
Methods
• Statistical methods: z-score, interquartile range, and Grubbs’ test.
• Distance methods: identify points far from neighbours or cluster centres.
• Machine-learning methods: isolation forest, k-nearest neighbours, and one-class methods.
Handling outliers
An outlier should not be deleted automatically. First understand its context. It may be removed if it is a confirmed data-entry
error, treated separately if it is a genuine rare class, or retained if it carries important business information.

12. Dimensionality Reduction
Dimensionality reduction reduces the number of variables while trying to preserve important information. It can reduce
computation, noise, storage, visualization difficulty, and overfitting.
Two components
1. Feature selection: select a useful subset of the original variables. Filter methods use statistical properties, wrapper
methods test subsets using a model, and embedded methods select features during model training.
2. Feature extraction: transform many original variables into a smaller set of new variables or components.
PYQ focus: 2019 — define dimensionality reduction and explain feature selection and feature extraction.
13. Snowflake Schema
A snowflake schema is an extension of the star schema. Dimension tables are normalized into additional related tables,
producing a branching structure that resembles a snowflake.
Fact: Sales / | \n Product Time Location | | Category table City → State → Country
Advantages
• Reduces redundant values in dimension tables.
• May save storage space.
• Represents hierarchical relationships clearly.
• Can make dimension maintenance easier.
Limitations
• More tables require more joins.
• Queries may be slower than a denormalized star schema.
• Design and maintenance are more complex.
PYQ focus: 2019 — explain snowflake schema with diagram, benefits, and limitations.
14. Advanced Databases
Centralized database
A centralized database stores the database and application processing at a central computing facility. Users at different
locations connect to the central system through communication links. Validation, verification, and transaction processing are
mainly performed centrally.
End-user database
An end-user database is designed for direct use by end users or managers. It often provides summarized or analytical
information rather than detailed transaction processing. Different users may access shared data according to their roles and
needs.
PYQ focus: 2018 — discuss any two advanced databases.

16. Databases in Knowledge Management
Benefits
• Efficient storage and retrieval of organizational knowledge.
• Improved consistency and accuracy through controlled design.
• Centralized repository for shared knowledge.
• Security and access control through authentication and roles.
• Scalability as users and knowledge grow.
• Support for data-driven decision-making using past experience and best practices.
Limitations
• Design and maintenance can be complex and resource-intensive.
• Poor organization can create information overload.
• Keeping knowledge current is difficult.
• Employees may resist documenting or sharing knowledge.
• Traditional relational databases may not handle unstructured documents, videos, emails, and scanned files naturally.
