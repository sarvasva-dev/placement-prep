2. Meaning of Knowledge Management
Knowledge Management (KM) is a systematic process of creating, acquiring, organizing, storing, sharing, applying, and
improving knowledge so that an organization can achieve its objectives more effectively.
KM is not simply the act of storing documents. A successful KM programme makes the right knowledge available to the right
person, at the right time, in a usable form. It connects people, processes, technology, and organizational culture.
Simple definition for examination
Knowledge Management is the planned and systematic management of organizational knowledge to improve learning,
decision-making, innovation, performance, and long-term organizational success.
Why KM is needed
1. Employees may leave, but their experience should remain available to the organization.
2. Organizations generate large quantities of documents, reports, emails, designs, and lessons.
3. Repeated mistakes can be reduced by sharing previous experience.
4. Employees can solve problems faster when they can find relevant knowledge.
5. Knowledge sharing supports innovation and continuous improvement.
6. Managers can make better decisions when knowledge is organized and reliable.

Explicit knowledge
Explicit knowledge is documented and easy to store, communicate, and reproduce. Examples include manuals, policies,
reports, formulas, databases, diagrams, code, and recorded procedures.
Tacit knowledge
Tacit knowledge exists in the experience, skills, intuition, and judgement of people. It is difficult to write completely. Examples
include a senior technician’s ability to diagnose a machine by sound or a manager’s experience in negotiating with a supplier.
KM challenge
A major challenge is converting useful tacit knowledge into shareable explicit knowledge without losing its context. Interviews,
mentoring, storytelling, demonstrations, communities of practice, and lessons-learned sessions help in this conversion.
4. Knowledge Management Life Cycle
The KM life cycle describes the movement of knowledge from creation to application and improvement.
1. Knowledge creation or acquisition: Develop new knowledge through research, experience, experimentation, customers,
partners, and external sources.
2. Knowledge capture: Identify useful knowledge and record it through documents, interviews, databases, videos, or expert
systems.
3. Knowledge organization: Classify, tag, index, and arrange knowledge so users can find it.
4. Knowledge storage: Preserve knowledge in repositories, databases, document systems, portals, or cloud platforms.
5. Knowledge sharing: Transfer knowledge through portals, email, collaboration tools, communities, training, and mentoring.
6. Knowledge application: Use knowledge for decisions, problem solving, product development, customer service, and
operations.
7. Knowledge evaluation and improvement: Check accuracy, relevance, usage, and results; then update or retire
knowledge.
Create → Capture → Organize → Store → Share → Apply → Evaluate → Improve
Example
After a server failure, an IT team conducts a review. It records the cause, symptoms, commands used, recovery steps, and
preventive actions. The document is indexed in the incident repository. A future engineer searches it and solves a similar
problem faster. This demonstrates the full KM cycle.
5. Knowledge Management System (KMS)
A Knowledge Management System is an information system designed to support the creation, capture, storage, retrieval,
sharing, and application of knowledge within an organization.
Objectives of a KMS

• Reduce duplication of work.
• Preserve expert knowledge.
• Support faster and better decisions.

7. People, Process and Technology
People
People are the central part of KM. They create knowledge, solve problems, validate information, teach colleagues, and decide
how knowledge should be used.
Processes
Processes define how knowledge is captured, reviewed, approved, classified, shared, updated, archived, and measured. A
process avoids random document storage and creates consistency.
Technology
Technology supports repositories, search, collaboration, workflow, analytics, AI-based recommendations, document
management, and access control.

8. Knowledge Repositories and Content Types
A knowledge repository is an organized place where knowledge is stored and made available to authorized users.
Examples of repositories
• Document management system.
• Intranet and KM portal.
• Knowledge base and frequently asked questions.
• Lessons-learned repository.
• Best-practice library.
• Customer-support ticket archive.
• Project and software code repository.
• Training and e-learning system.
• Expert directory.
• Data warehouse and analytical repository.
• Wiki and collaborative documentation.
Content types
• Structured knowledge: tables, records, classifications, rules, and metadata.
• Semi-structured knowledge: emails, forms, spreadsheets, and reports.
• Unstructured knowledge: videos, audio, scanned documents, conversations, and expert narratives.
9. Knowledge Capture Techniques
Knowledge capture means identifying and recording useful knowledge before it is lost or forgotten.
Major techniques
1. Interviews: Speak with subject experts and document their methods, decisions, and experiences.
2. Observation: Watch experts perform real work and record the steps and conditions.
3. Think-aloud method: Ask an expert to explain the reasoning while solving a problem.
4. Document analysis: Extract knowledge from manuals, reports, policies, emails, and project files.
5. Lessons-learned meetings: Record what went well, what failed, why it happened, and what should change.
6. After-action reviews: Review an activity immediately after completion.
7. Expert-system elicitation: Convert facts and rules from experts into a knowledge base.
8. Communities of practice: Allow practitioners to exchange experience regularly.
9. Storytelling: Preserve context and practical judgement through real examples.

10. Knowledge Codification and Organization
Knowledge codification converts knowledge into an organized and reusable form. It may involve writing a procedure, defining
a rule, creating metadata, assigning tags, building a decision tree, or creating a searchable FAQ.
Codification steps
1. Identify the purpose and target users.
2. Select the appropriate format: document, checklist, rule, video, diagram, FAQ, or database record.
3. Remove unnecessary duplication and ambiguity.
4. Add title, author, owner, version, date, subject, keywords, and access level.
5. Review the content with a subject expert.
6. Publish it in the correct repository.
7. Set a review date and update responsibility.
Taxonomy and metadata
A taxonomy is a structured classification of subjects. Metadata is data about knowledge, such as author, date, department,
version, location, confidentiality, and keywords. Good taxonomy and metadata improve search and reduce information
overload.
11. Knowledge Sharing Techniques
• Communities of practice: Groups of people with a shared professional interest who learn from one another.
• Mentoring and coaching: Experienced employees guide less experienced employees.
• Peer assist: A team asks another team for advice before starting a task.
• Job rotation: Employees learn different roles and build broader understanding.
• Workshops and seminars: Experts explain methods, tools, and lessons.
• Internal social networks: Employees ask questions and share updates.
• Knowledge portals: Centralized access to documents, experts, FAQs, and learning.
• Storytelling: Real events communicate context and practical lessons.
• Collaboration tools: Chat, shared documents, issue trackers, and project workspaces.
• Recognition and incentives: Employees are encouraged to contribute useful knowledge.
12. Knowledge Application
Knowledge has value only when it is applied. Application means using knowledge in decisions, problem solving, innovation,
customer service, operations, and learning.
Examples

14. Appreciation and Importance of KM
Appreciation of KM means understanding its value and recognizing how it contributes to organizational performance.
Benefits
1. Better decision-making: Managers can use reliable previous knowledge and current information.
2. Faster problem solving: Employees do not need to start from zero.
3. Preservation of expertise: Important experience remains after employees leave.
4. Improved innovation: Shared ideas combine to create new products and services.
5. Improved efficiency: Reusable procedures reduce duplicated work.
6. Better customer service: Employees receive consistent and accurate answers.
7. Organizational learning: The organization learns from success and failure.
8. Quality improvement: Best practices and standards reduce variation.
9. Risk reduction: Lessons and controls reduce repeated mistakes.
10. Competitive advantage: Valuable knowledge can help an organization perform better than competitors.
KM and innovation
Innovation requires ideas, experimentation, feedback, and learning. KM helps people find previous work, combine knowledge
from different fields, identify gaps, and share new ideas. It prevents both unnecessary repetition and accidental loss of useful
discoveries.
KM and decision-making

15. Limitations and Challenges of KM
Cultural challenges
• Employees may not want to share knowledge because they fear losing personal importance.
• There may be a “knowledge is power” attitude.
• Employees may not trust management or the system.
• Contributions may be ignored if recognition is absent.
• Different departments may protect information instead of collaborating.
Technical challenges
• Poor search makes useful knowledge difficult to find.
• Systems may not integrate with existing databases and applications.
• Unstructured content is difficult to classify automatically.
• Migration from old systems can create errors and missing context.
• Downtime, cyberattacks, or data loss can affect availability.
Quality challenges
• Knowledge may be outdated or duplicated.
• Wrong advice can spread quickly through a shared repository.
• Different experts may provide conflicting recommendations.
• Information overload can reduce usefulness.
• Quality and ownership may not be clearly assigned.
Financial and managerial challenges
• Implementation, training, integration, and maintenance can be expensive.
• Benefits may be difficult to measure immediately.
• Senior management support may decline if early results are not visible.
• A KM project may fail if it is treated only as an IT project.
Privacy, ethical, and security challenges
• Sensitive employee, customer, financial, or intellectual-property knowledge may be exposed.
• Knowledge may be used beyond the purpose for which it was collected.
• Access must be limited according to role and need.
• AI-generated summaries or recommendations may contain errors or bias.
• The organization must respect legal, contractual, and ethical obligations.

17. KM Governance and Security
KM governance defines ownership, standards, roles, policies, review cycles, access rights, and accountability.
Important governance rules
1. Assign an owner to every important knowledge asset.
2. Use role-based access control.
3. Maintain version history.
4. Record creation and review dates.
5. Apply retention and archival rules.
6. Review sensitive knowledge regularly.
7. Separate draft, approved, and obsolete content.
8. Maintain audit logs for important changes.
9. Define a process for correction and reporting of wrong information.
10. Train users on privacy, security, copyright, and ethical use.
18. Implementation Roadmap for a KMS
1. Identify business problems: Start with a real need such as repeated support issues, slow onboarding, or loss of expert
knowledge.
2. Assess existing knowledge: Find repositories, experts, documents, processes, and gaps.
3. Define objectives and scope: Select a manageable department or use case.
4. Design taxonomy and governance: Define categories, metadata, ownership, review, access, and quality rules.
5. Select technology: Choose a portal, repository, search tool, collaboration platform, workflow, or AI feature.
6. Capture and clean content: Remove duplicates, correct errors, convert valuable tacit knowledge, and prepare content.
7. Pilot the system: Test with a small group and collect feedback.
8. Train and motivate users: Explain value, provide training, and recognize contributions.
9. Measure and improve: Track usage, results, unanswered questions, and content quality.
10. Scale gradually: Add departments, integrations, and advanced analytics after the foundation works.
Business need → Assessment → Scope → Governance → Technology → Content → Pilot → Adoption →
Measurement → Scale
