2. Executive Information System (EIS) and Executive Support
System (ESS)
An Executive Information System (EIS), also called an Executive Support System (ESS), is a specialized information system
designed to support senior executives. It provides easy access to important internal and external information required for
strategic decisions.
EIS presents information in a simple form such as dashboards, graphs, scorecards, trends, alerts, and summarized reports.
It is designed for executives who need a quick understanding of organizational performance rather than detailed transaction
records.
Main characteristics
• User-friendly interface with simple menus and dashboards.
• Summarized information focused on KPIs, trends, exceptions, and performance indicators.
• Real-time or near-real-time access to important information.
• Graphical presentation through charts, graphs, heat maps, and scorecards.
• Drill-down capability: an executive can move from a summary figure to detailed data.
• Trend analysis and forecasting using historical and current data.
• Integration of internal data with external information such as competitors, market trends, government policies, and
economic indicators.

Main functionalities
• Strategic decision support for investments, mergers, market entry, restructuring, and long-term planning.
• Performance monitoring using sales, profit margin, market share, productivity, and other KPIs.
• Environmental scanning to identify opportunities and threats.
• Personalized dashboards for different executives.
• Data mining and predictive analysis.
• Quick access to critical information during urgent or crisis situations.
Example: A CFO may view revenue, costs, cash flow, and profit margins; a marketing head may view sales, customer

3. Decision Support System (DSS)
A Decision Support System is a computer-based information system that helps managers make decisions, especially
semi-structured and unstructured decisions. It combines data, analytical models, and an interactive user interface.
Steps in constructing a DSS
1. Identification of the problem: Define scope, extent, resources, expected return on investment, risks, and possible
conceptual solutions.
2. Decision about the mode of development: Select programming language, development shell, software tools, and
technologies suitable for the problem.
3. Development of a prototype: Build an initial working model using available knowledge and interact with domain experts.
4. Prototype validation: Test the prototype, identify weaknesses, and revise it.
5. Planning for a full-scale system: Plan subsystems, assign responsibilities, prepare schedules, and decide
implementation resources.
6. Final implementation, maintenance, and evaluation: Implement the complete system, train users, maintain it, and
evaluate its performance.
Role of DSS in business
• What-if analysis: Studies the effect of changing input variables.
• Goal-oriented analysis: Determines input values needed to achieve a desired goal.
• Risk analysis: Compares alternatives according to low, medium, or high risk.
• Model building: Represents relationships among variables, assumptions, and constraints.
• Graphical analysis: Uses charts to summarize data, show trends, and compare alternatives.
PYQ focus: 2016 — DSS and its help in business; 2020 — EIS and DSS; 2023–24 — steps in constructing DSS and role in
business.

5. Artificial Intelligence (AI)
Artificial Intelligence is a branch of computer science concerned with creating systems that perform tasks requiring
human-like intelligence. Such tasks include learning, reasoning, problem solving, perception, language understanding,
planning, and decision-making.
Core capabilities of AI
• Knowledge representation.
• Reasoning.
• Problem solving.
• Learning.
• Perception.
• Natural-language understanding.
• Planning.
• Object manipulation and movement.
Goals of AI in knowledge management
1. Automate knowledge capture from documents, emails, databases, and unstructured data.
2. Improve knowledge discovery by finding patterns, relationships, and trends.
3. Support knowledge sharing and collaboration by recommending documents, experts, and communities.
4. Personalize knowledge delivery according to user role, interests, and learning behaviour.
5. Improve search and retrieval using semantic search and knowledge graphs.
6. Automate knowledge maintenance by finding outdated, inconsistent, or incorrect information.
7. Enable predictive analytics for future trends, risks, and proactive actions.
Applications of AI
• Game playing: Systems search possible moves and use heuristics to select actions.
• Speech recognition: Converts spoken language into text or commands.
• Natural-language understanding: Interprets language in a particular domain.
• Computer vision: Extracts meaning from images and video.
• Heuristic classification: Places an object or situation into a category, such as approving a suspicious transaction.

7. Multidimensional Analysis and OLAP
Multidimensional analysis examines data from multiple perspectives or dimensions, such as time, product, region, customer,
department, or sales channel. It is widely used in data warehouses and OLAP systems.
OLAP enables users to extract and view data from different points of view. A relational table is commonly viewed as
two-dimensional, but a multidimensional model organizes measures across several dimensions.
OLAP cube
An OLAP cube is a multidimensional data structure. Each cell contains a measure, such as sales, profit, cost, quantity, or
budget, at the intersection of dimension members.

8. Data Warehouse
A data warehouse is a subject-oriented, integrated, time-variant, and non-volatile collection of data designed to support
decision-making. It provides a central and consistent view of current and historical information.
Four classic characteristics
• Subject-oriented: Organized around important subjects such as customers, sales, products, or finance.
• Integrated: Data from different sources is cleaned, standardized, and combined.
• Time-variant: Historical data is stored with a time dimension for trend analysis.
• Non-volatile: Once loaded, data is mainly read and analysed rather than frequently changed like operational data.
Why a data warehouse is needed
1. Centralized storage of data from multiple sources.
2. Improved data quality and consistency through cleansing and transformation.
3. Support for complex analytical queries and decision-making.
4. Historical analysis and trend identification.
5. Scalability for very large data volumes.
6. Integration of data from different formats and systems.
7. Support for business intelligence, dashboards, and reporting.
8. Better query performance without disturbing daily transaction systems.
ETL process
Operational databases / external sources I Extract I Transform and clean I Load I Data warehouse I
OLAP / BI / reports
ETL means Extract, Transform, and Load. Data is extracted from source systems, cleaned and transformed into a common
structure, and loaded into the warehouse. Refresh processes update the warehouse periodically or continuously according
to the design.
PYQ focus: 2019 — three-tier architecture and characteristics; 2020 — data warehouse architecture; 2023–24 — characteristics

10. Data Mart
A data mart is a smaller, subject-specific subset of organizational data designed for a particular department, user group, or
business function. A sales data mart may contain product, customer, order, and revenue information.
Characteristics and benefits
• Smaller than an enterprise data warehouse.
• Focused on one department or subject.
• Can be developed in weeks rather than months or years.
• Usually less expensive to implement.
• Easier for users to understand.
• Can provide faster reporting because fewer data is queried.
• Can be customized for departmental requirements.
• Can be deployed on smaller or distributed servers.
• Supports incremental development.

11. Business Intelligence (BI) in Data Warehousing
Business Intelligence is a collection of strategies, technologies, applications, data, and processes used to analyse business
information and convert it into actionable insight.

Importance of BI
• Combines internal data such as finance, sales, customers, and operations with external market data.
• Supports operational decisions such as pricing and product positioning.
• Supports strategic decisions such as goals, priorities, investment, and direction.
• Provides dashboards, reports, alerts, and visual analysis.
• Helps identify trends, patterns, risks, and opportunities.
• Improves the quality and speed of managerial decisions.
Data warehousing and BI work together. The warehouse supplies cleaned, integrated, and historical data, while BI tools
provide reports, dashboards, OLAP analysis, and data visualizations. The combined approach is often described as BI and
data warehousing.

12. Data Transformation
Data transformation is the process of converting, cleaning, restructuring, standardizing, and enriching data so that it
becomes suitable for analytics, reporting, storage, or decision-making.
Common transformation activities
• Changing data types and formats.
• Removing duplicate records.
• Handling missing values.
• Standardizing names, units, dates, and codes.
• Combining data from multiple sources.
• Aggregating daily data into monthly or yearly data.
• Filtering irrelevant records.
• Creating calculated fields and derived measures.
• Applying validation rules.
The goal is to make data accurate, consistent, accessible, understandable, and actionable. AI and machine-learning tools
can automate parts of data transformation.
PYQ focus: 2025–26 — explain data transformation.
13. Business Expert System (BES)
A Business Expert System is a knowledge-based information system based on artificial intelligence. It stores expert
knowledge about a specific problem domain and provides advice or recommendations to users.
An expert system is interactive. It can answer questions, apply rules to facts, and explain the reasoning behind its
conclusion. It is useful when a problem is complex and a simple algorithm cannot provide a satisfactory answer.

14. Top-Down and Bottom-Up Warehouse Design
Two common approaches are used to build a data warehouse: top-down and bottom-up.
Bottom-up approach
In the bottom-up approach, data marts are created first for individual business areas such as sales, finance, or inventory.
These data marts are later integrated to form an enterprise data warehouse. Shared dimensions are called conformed
dimensions.
Advantages of bottom-up
• Data marts can be delivered quickly.
• Reports can be generated early.
• Development is incremental.
• New departments can be added by creating more data marts.
• Initial cost and scope may be smaller.
Disadvantages of bottom-up
• Different marts may become inconsistent if common standards are not enforced.
• Integration can become difficult later.
• The overall enterprise view may take longer to achieve.

Top-down approach
In the top-down approach, the enterprise data warehouse is built first. Departmental data marts are then created from the
integrated warehouse.
Advantages of top-down
• Provides consistent dimensions and definitions.
• Creates an integrated enterprise view.
• Data marts inherit standardized warehouse data.
• Robust for organization-wide strategic reporting.
Disadvantages of top-down
• Large initial project.
• Higher cost and longer implementation time.
• Less flexible for immediate departmental needs.
• Benefits may appear later.
